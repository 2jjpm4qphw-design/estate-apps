The Reading Room

How this works

Roadcraft measures how you move the car — nothing else. It cannot see other traffic, the car in front, or the road ahead. Everything here is reviewed when parked.

Starting a drive

Tap Start drive. That arms the accelerometer and GPS together; after the first time it re-arms silently on every open. End it with a 1.4-second hold on the button — deliberate, so it can't happen by accident.

Calibration is stored per vehicle and survives forever. You only redo it if the phone mount moves.

Calibration

The phone doesn't know which way it's facing relative to the car, so two things get established.

Mount calibration finds which way is down. Sit still, parked, for five seconds. Once set, that reference is frozen and only re-levels when the car is genuinely still — otherwise a long pull away would be absorbed into "gravity" and vanish.

Forward axis finds which way is forwards. It learns automatically by correlating accelerometer readings against GPS speed changes, or you can set it in ten seconds with Capture forward axis manually: tap it stopped, then pull away firmly in a straight line.

The three drive views

G-force — a traction circle. Up is acceleration, down is braking, left and right are cornering. The lime trail shows recent history, the ring shows where you are now. Rings mark 0.25 g intervals to 1.0 g.

Telemetry — six live bars. Green ends at 60% of the event threshold, amber at the threshold, red above. The bar warns you before something counts against you. The white tick is peak-hold: the worst value so far this drive.

Map — rotates so the road ahead is at the top. The lime arrow is you; the compass shows where north has gone. It freezes rotation below 5 mph, because a stationary GPS heading is noise.

The six telemetry channels

Accelerationg Brakingg Corneringg, left/right Grip usedg, combined Yaw rate°/s Input smoothnessm/s³

Grip used is the vector sum of braking/acceleration and cornering — the actual demand on the tyres however you got there. It's the number that matters on a wet roundabout.

Yaw rate is how fast the car is rotating. It's also what stops potholes being counted as corners.

Input smoothness is jerk, the rate of change of acceleration. Low jerk means you read the road early.

Modes

A mode describes how strictly you are judged — not what kind of journey it is. It sets the thresholds and how much each event costs, and is stored per vehicle.

For a mixed run — lanes out of Harpenden, motorway, then across town — use Road. Careful's thresholds are tight enough that correct defensive braking in city traffic gets logged as a fault, which teaches you nothing. Switching between Road on weekdays and Spirited at the weekend is fine: the trend chart only ever compares drives recorded in the same mode, so you get two clean series rather than one muddled average.

Spirited barely scores acceleration — hard acceleration in a straight line on a clear road says more about the car than the driver. Braking weight does not drop in Spirited, deliberately. Hard braking means the road ahead surprised you, and no car justifies relaxing that.

The scores

Smoothness — starts at 100, subtracts a penalty for each event scaled per 10 miles, so a short trip isn't punished. Under 0.3 miles it shows nothing at all.

Anticipation — the ratio of gentle early decelerations to hard late ones. High means you were coming off the throttle in good time. Low means the brakes were doing work your eyes should have done.

Corner discipline — the share of your cornering time that also carried brake. Braking should be finished before turn-in; the tyres can do one job well or two badly. Under 12% is tidy.

What counts as an event

Three tests, all of which must pass:

  1. Exceed the threshold for your mode
  2. Sustain it for 280 ms — a driver input lasts that long, a pothole lasts twenty milliseconds
  3. Be above 5 mph, and for cornering, show real yaw

Both acceleration channels are low-pass filtered at about 2 Hz first. Vehicle dynamics happen below that; road texture and suspension happen above.

Speed limits

Roads come from OpenStreetMap, cached locally, matched to the nearest one weighted by how well its bearing agrees with your heading. The match radius scales with your GPS accuracy.

Solid red ring — a limit tagged in OSM
Dashed ring — assumed from road type
Blue roundel — a minimum limit, information only

Assumed figures use the statutory defaults: motorway 70, single carriageway 60, residential 30, and the street-lighting rule — a road with a system of lighting is 30 unless signed otherwise. These are inferences, and they will be wrong wherever a lower limit is signed but untagged.

The signs are the authority. Always. This will be wrong on slip roads, parallel roads, roadworks and every smart-motorway gantry.

Fuel and tyres — both are models

Neither is measured. There is no connection to the car.

Fuel is calculated from physics: the energy needed to push a known mass against rolling resistance and air, plus the energy spent accelerating it. That is converted to fuel using an engine model with a fixed efficiency term and a running-loss term — the second is why town driving costs so much more per mile than a cruise. Parameters were fitted against published real-world figures, then you scale the whole thing with a brim-to-brim fill-up in Setup.

The number worth reading is not mpg, it is waste: the share of energy you put into the car and then turned into brake heat. Accelerating towards something you then have to stop for. Under 15% is efficient; over 30% means a lot of wasted work.

Tyre load distributes measured force to each corner. Cornering loads the outside pair, braking loads the front, driving loads whichever axle drives. Wear rises faster than force, so the square is used — good for ranking drives against each other, meaningless as tread depth. It cannot see pressure, compound, or alignment, and misalignment is the commonest cause of genuinely uneven wear. What it is good for is spotting a persistent one-sided bias across many drives, which is worth a tracking check.

Conditions

Each drive is tagged wet or dry with a temperature, fetched from Open-Meteo when the drive ends. Wet drives are marked in the list. Your smoothness in November rain is not comparable to a dry August evening, and grip demand should visibly fall when the road is wet — the g-g plot will show whether yours actually does.

Backing up

Everything lives in this browser. Safari clears storage for sites left unopened for about a week, which would take every drive, your calibration and your settings with it. Back up from Setup and keep the file somewhere real. Restoring merges by drive, so nothing recorded since the backup is lost, and it asks separately before touching calibration or settings.

Driving instructor

Off by default. When on, it comments aloud on what you have just done — and says nothing the rest of the time, which is most of the time.

It only speaks when an event is 15% or more past the threshold for your mode, so a marginal event passes without comment. It defers while combined g is above 0.32, because talking to someone mid-corner is the opposite of helpful. It will not speak within nine seconds of a speed limit or camera warning, and not below 8 mph.

Praise is deliberately hard to earn and gets rarer as the drive goes on: three clean minutes for the first, six for the second, ten for the third, and then it stops offering unprompted compliments altogether. Acknowledging a recovery after a rough patch stays available, because that one actually tells you something. A motorway cruise will never be complimented for doing nothing.

Everything is past tense. Events are only recognised once they finish, so it is describing the corner you just took — never predicting the one ahead. It cannot see the road, so it will never warn you about anything coming up.

Speed cameras

Cameras come from OpenStreetMap, cached alongside the road data. A warning fires once per camera per drive, and only for cameras inside a 50° cone ahead of you — so the one on the opposite carriageway stays quiet. Where OSM records which way a camera faces, that's respected too.

Warning distance scales with speed, about 13 seconds ahead: roughly 180 m in town, up to 700 m at motorway speeds. The badge turns solid inside 250 m.

Coverage is incomplete and always will be. Mobile camera vans appear in no database. A new fixed camera may take months to be tagged. No warning means nothing at all — never treat silence as clear road.

GPS camera-location data is legal in the UK. Radar and laser jammers are not; this app does neither, it only reads a map.

The blue-grey dot at the end of a route is simply where you stopped. Jerky inputs are never plotted — there are too many to read. On the Recurring spots map, circle size means number of events, not severity.

On the live map, Roadcraft only ever draws in lime — the arrow for you, the trail for your route. Anything else you can see is OpenStreetMap's own cartography, not this app. Small pale-blue rectangles among houses are usually swimming pools; larger blue shapes are ponds and lakes. If you want to know what something is, find it on openstreetmap.org and tap it.

Recurring spots

Harsh braking and cornering clustered within 60 m across every drive you've recorded, against how many times you passed that point. A spot needs at least three events across two separate drives before it appears, so one bad morning won't create one.

This is the most useful thing here. A score tells you how a drive went; a recurring spot tells you about a specific corner you keep getting wrong.

What it cannot do

No following distance, no other traffic, no mirror checks, no fuel. iOS suspends the app when the screen locks or you switch away, so it only records while it's open and in front. Speed limits need a data connection.

Holding a phone while driving is an offence in the UK, and glancing at a mounted screen can still be careless driving. Every control locks above 3 mph for that reason.

Warnings

Siri's voices can't be used here. Apple doesn't expose them to web apps at all — no browser on any device can reach them, so this isn't something the app can add.

The best available substitute is a downloaded high-quality voice: Settings › Accessibility › Spoken Content › Voices › English › Daniel, then choose the Premium or Enhanced download. It appears in the list above once installed and sounds dramatically better than the compact default.

Driving instructor

Silence is the normal state. It only speaks when something is clearly over the threshold — not marginal — and it stays quiet while you are actually busy cornering or braking, and for nine seconds after any limit or camera warning.

Praise needs clean minutes that contained real cornering, so a motorway straight earns nothing — and the bar rises each time, stopping after three. Recovery after a rough patch is always acknowledged. Criticism is past tense: an event is only recognised once it has finished, so it is telling you about the corner you just took, not the one you are in.

A voice is still a distraction even without a screen. If it starts feeling like company rather than coaching, turn it off.

Roadcraft has no idea what the speed limit is, where other traffic is, or how close you are to the car in front. It measures how you move the car, nothing else. Read the road and the signs — this app is reviewed when parked.
