Form figures
Read left to right, oldest run first. Each digit is a finishing position; 0 means it finished outside the top nine. P pulled up, F fell, U unseated, R refused, B brought down.

A - marks a new season. A / marks a longer absence.

So 85-3106 means: last season it ran 8th then 5th; this season 3rd, 1st, 0 (out of the frame), and most recently 6th.
The going
The state of the ground. Horses have real surface preferences, so a horse whose wins all came on soft is a genuine negative on fast ground.
  • Turf, soft to fast: Heavy → Soft → Good to Soft → Good → Good to Firm → Firm
  • All-weather: Slow → Standard to Slow → Standard → Fast
Heavy ground is a stamina test and suits big, powerful horses. Good to firm favours speed. The GoingStick reading gives a number — roughly 6 or below is soft, 8 or above is firm.
Odds and implied probability
Fractional 5/1 = £5 profit per £1 staked. Decimal 6.0 = total return per £1 including the stake. Same thing.

Implied probability = 1 ÷ decimal odds. 6.0 means the price says one win in six, or 16.7%.

Add up the implied probabilities of every runner and you'll get more than 100%. That excess is the bookmaker's margin — the overround. It's why the market beats most people over time, and why the only real question is whether your estimate of a horse's chance beats the price.
Handicaps and the Official Rating
The BHA handicapper gives every horse an OR. In a handicap the ratings set the weights — the better horse carries more lead — with the stated aim that every runner would dead-heat.

So in a handicap you aren't hunting the best horse. You're hunting the horse the handicapper has got wrong: usually one that's lightly raced and improving faster than its mark.

Roughly, 1lb ≈ half a length over a mile. Non-handicaps (maidens, Group and Listed races) run at set weights, so the best horse should simply win — which is why favourites there are short and rarely good value.
Reading the rest of the racecard
  • Number in brackets after the saddlecloth — the draw, which starting stall. Matters a lot in sprints, much less over a mile.
  • Small number after the horse's name — days since it last ran. A long layoff is a real question mark; 14–40 days is a normal turnaround.
  • Weight like 9-7 — nine stone seven pounds.
  • Headgear — b blinkers, v visor, h hood, p cheek pieces, t tongue strap. A 1 after it means first time, which is the trainer signalling a change.
  • (3) or (7) after a jockey — an apprentice claim, that many pounds off the weight.
  • BF — beaten favourite last time out.
  • WS — has had wind surgery. WS2 is the second run since.
  • Class 1 to 7 — Class 1 is Group and Listed racing, Class 7 the lowest. A horse dropping in class is easier; rising is harder.
Bet types and place terms
Win — the horse must win. Each-way — two bets of equal size, one on the win and one on the place, so a £2 each-way bet costs £4.

Place terms depend on field size and are set by the bookmaker. Typical:
  • 2–4 runners — win only
  • 5–7 runners — 2 places at 1/4 the odds
  • 8+ runners — 3 places at 1/5 the odds
  • Big handicaps of 16+ — 4 places at 1/4
In small fields each-way is usually poor value, because you're paying a second stake for an outcome that was already likely.

Why this app asks what you think the chance is
Because it's the only question that matters, and the only one you can get better at.

Whether a bet wins is mostly noise over a handful of races. Whether your probability estimates are calibrated — whether the horses you call 25% shots actually win about a quarter of the time — is a skill, and it shows up in the numbers far faster than profit does.

The Reckoning tab compares the wins you expected against the wins you got. If those two numbers track each other, you're reading form properly, whatever the balance says.

Everything is stored on this device only. Nothing is sent anywhere. This app tracks what you bet — it will never tell you to bet more, and it has no way to add money to anything.
